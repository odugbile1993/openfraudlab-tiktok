## In a nutshell

A distribution shows how often each value appears, and a histogram is the quickest way to see it. In a normal distribution about 68%, 95% and 99.7% of values fall within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the mean, but much real data is skewed.

## Key ideas

- **Distribution:** How often each value shows up — The overall shape of a column
- **Histogram:** Histogram: values grouped into bins — Taller bar = more rows in that bin
- **Normal curve:** Symmetric and bell-shaped — Mean, median and mode sit together in the middle
- **68–95–99.7 rule:** ~68% within 1 SD / ~95% within 2 SD / ~99.7% within 3 SD — SD = standard deviation
- **Skewed data:** Real data is often lopsided — Incomes, transaction amounts: long tail to the right
- **Takeaway:** Plot the distribution before you trust a summary.

## Try it yourself

Ask 15 friends or classmates how many hours they slept last night (or make a list from memory). Draw a histogram on paper with 1-hour bins and decide whether the shape looks roughly symmetric or skewed.

Data Science from Scratch, lesson six: distributions, and the normal curve.

A distribution describes how often each value, or range of values, appears in your data. It's the overall shape of a column, not just its average.

The easiest way to see a distribution is a histogram. You group values into bins, and the height of each bar shows how many rows fall into that bin.

One famous shape is the normal distribution: a symmetric bell curve. Most values cluster around the centre, and in a perfect normal curve, the mean, median, and mode are all the same.

For a normal distribution there's a handy rule. About sixty-eight percent of values fall within one standard deviation of the mean, about ninety-five percent within two, and about ninety-nine point seven percent within three.

But plenty of real data isn't normal. Incomes and transaction amounts are often right-skewed: lots of small values and a long tail of large ones. In that case the mean usually sits above the median.

So the takeaway: plot your data first. The shape tells you which summaries and methods make sense.
