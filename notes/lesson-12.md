## In a nutshell

Correlation measures how two variables move together (Pearson's r runs from -1 to +1), but it does not show cause. A hidden third factor, reverse causation or pure chance can all produce correlations; testing cause usually needs an experiment or careful study design.

## Key ideas

- **Correlation:** Two things that tend to move together — Pearson's r runs from -1 to +1
- **The trap:** Moving together doesn't mean one causes the other
- **Hidden factor:** A third factor can drive both — Ice cream sales and drownings both rise in hot weather
- **Wrong direction:** The cause can run the other way — Failed payments lead to support calls, not the reverse
- **Chance:** Some correlations are pure coincidence — Compare enough columns and some will line up
- **Takeaway:** Correlation is a clue, not proof. — Testing cause usually needs an experiment

## Try it yourself

Think of two things that tend to rise together, for example umbrella sales and traffic jams. Write down a third factor that could drive both, then describe a simple comparison or experiment that could check whether one really causes the other.

Data Science from Scratch, lesson twelve: correlation is not causation.

Correlation means two things tend to move together. The most common measure, Pearson's r, runs from minus one to plus one. Plus one is a perfect positive straight-line link, minus one is a perfect negative one, and zero means no straight-line link.

The trap is assuming that because two things move together, one must cause the other. Correlation alone can't tell you that.

Often a third factor drives both. A classic example: ice cream sales and drownings both rise in hot weather. Ice cream doesn't cause drowning. The heat drives both.

Sometimes the cause runs the other way. Customers who call support more may have more failed payments. The calls probably don't cause the failures. The failures cause the calls.

And some correlations are just chance. If you compare enough columns, some will line up by coincidence.

So the takeaway: treat correlation as a clue, not proof. To test cause and effect, you usually need a controlled experiment, or a carefully designed study.
